What is ERCP?

ERCP (Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography) is an advanced endoscopic procedure that allows the physician to explore and treat diseases of the bile ducts and pancreas without surgery. It combines upper digestive endoscopy with real-time X-ray imaging (fluoroscopy).

The endoscope is introduced through the mouth into the duodenum. A thin catheter is then inserted into the bile duct to inject contrast dye, making the biliary tree visible on screen. The physician can then act directly: extract stones, dilate a stricture, or place a biliary stent.

Key benefit: ERCP treats bile duct diseases in a single session, without any incision, with discharge within 24 hours. It replaces open or laparoscopic abdominal surgery in the vast majority of cases.

Who needs ERCP?

Your physician may recommend ERCP if you have one of the following conditions:

Common bile duct stones

Gallstones lodged in the common bile duct, causing pain, jaundice or fever (cholangitis).

Obstructive jaundice

Yellow skin and eyes (jaundice) caused by a blocked bile duct from a stone, tumor or stricture.

Biliary strictures

Narrowing of the bile duct — benign (post-surgical, PSC) or malignant (pancreatic cancer, cholangiocarcinoma).

Biliary pancreatitis

Pancreatitis caused by a gallstone migrating into the pancreatic duct.

Pancreatic diseases

Chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic duct stones, Wirsung stricture, pancreatic fistulas.

Palliative biliary drainage

Biliary stent placement to drain bile in inoperable pancreatic or biliary tumors.

How is ERCP performed? — Step by step

  1. 1
    Pre-ERCP consultation

    Pr. Ait Errami meets you in consultation to explain the procedure, obtain your informed consent, prescribe blood tests and adjust your current medications (anticoagulants, etc.).

  2. 2
    Admission and preparation

    On the morning of the procedure, you are admitted to the Endoscopy Unit at CHU Mohammed VI. An intravenous line is placed. The anesthesiologist reviews your file and explains the sedation or general anesthesia.

  3. 3
    Anesthesia or deep sedation

    You are put to sleep (general anesthesia) or deeply sedated for your comfort. You feel nothing and will have no memory of the procedure.

  4. 4
    Insertion of the duodenoscope

    The endoscope is gently guided through the mouth, esophagus, and stomach into the duodenum. Under fluoroscopy, Pr. Ait Errami locates the opening of the papilla of Vater, where the bile and pancreatic ducts open.

  5. 5
    Cannulation and contrast injection

    A thin catheter is inserted into the bile duct. Contrast dye is injected, making the biliary tree visible in real time on the fluoroscopy screen. The physician confirms the diagnosis and precisely locates the problem.

  6. 6
    Therapeutic procedure

    Depending on the condition: sphincterotomy (cutting the sphincter to open the duct), stone extraction using a balloon or Dormia basket, balloon dilation of a stricture, or stent placement (plastic or metal biliary stent).

  7. 7
    Recovery room

    After the procedure (20–90 min), you are transferred to the recovery room. Monitoring continues for 2 to 6 hours: pulse, blood pressure and, if needed, pancreatic enzyme levels at 4 hours.

  8. 8
    Discharge

    If there are no complications, discharge is the same evening or the next morning. An escort is mandatory. Pr. Ait Errami gives you home monitoring instructions and warning signs to watch for.

How to prepare for ERCP

  • Strict fasting for 6 hours before the procedure — no food or drink (essential medications with a very small sip of water are permitted).
  • Blood tests: CBC, coagulation tests (PT, aPTT), blood group, creatinine, liver function tests. Required within 7 days before.
  • Anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents (Warfarin, Clopidogrel, Aspirin…): temporary suspension per Pr. Ait Errami's instructions. Never stop without medical advice.
  • Escort required: someone must drive you home after the procedure. You cannot leave alone or drive.
  • No driving on the day of the procedure due to residual sedation.
  • Documents to bring: health insurance card, recent blood test results, imaging reports (ultrasound, MRI, CT scan).
  • Report any known allergy, especially to iodine (contrast dye).

After ERCP: what to expect

In-hospital monitoring

2 to 6 hours in the recovery room. Vital signs monitored. Blood tests may be drawn 4 hours post-procedure to check pancreatic enzymes.

Resuming eating

Start with fluids, then light food progressively. Avoid fatty foods, alcohol and spicy food for 48 hours. Normal diet from day 2 if recovery is uneventful.

Going home

Discharge same evening or next morning. Light activity within 24–48 hours. Avoid strenuous physical effort for 5 days.

Warning signs — seek emergency care

Fever >38°C, severe or worsening abdominal pain, persistent nausea/vomiting, increasing jaundice, rectal bleeding: seek emergency care immediately or call +212 663 627 258.

Follow-up appointment

A follow-up consultation is scheduled 2 to 4 weeks after the procedure. Additional tests may be requested (liver function, ultrasound).

Risks and complications

ERCP is safe in experienced hands. As with any invasive procedure, there is a risk of complications. Pr. Ait Errami will discuss these in detail during your pre-ERCP consultation:

  • Post-ERCP pancreatitis (3–5%): inflammation of the pancreas, usually mild and resolving with a few days of medical treatment.
  • Bleeding after sphincterotomy (1–2%): usually minor and controlled endoscopically during the same session.
  • Cholangitis (biliary infection) (<1%): treatable with antibiotics and biliary drainage.
  • Duodenal perforation (<0.5%): rare, may require surgical management.
  • Allergic reaction to anesthesia or contrast dye: very rare, assessed during the pre-anesthesia consultation.
Important: The risk of leaving an untreated biliary condition (blocked stones, stricture) is generally greater than the risk of ERCP itself. The decision is always made after individualized benefit-risk assessment by Pr. Ait Errami.
